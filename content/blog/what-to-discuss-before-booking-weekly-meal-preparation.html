Weekly meal preparation turns a recurring question — "what's for dinner this week?" — into a fridge full of ready meals. The service works best when a few practical details are settled before the first cook, so the plan matches your household instead of a generic menu. This guide covers the points worth raising with your chef up front.

1. Number of people and meals per week

Start with the basics: how many people are you feeding, and how many meals do you actually want covered? A plan for two adults who want five dinners is different from one that also covers lunches or feeds a household of four. Being specific keeps the plan realistic — and keeps food from going to waste.

Think in servings, not just "meals." If leftovers are welcome, say so. If each person eats a different portion size, mention that too.

2. Allergies, dietary needs and hard avoidances

Allergies and medical restrictions are the first thing to flag. A shellfish allergy, a gluten intolerance, or a vegetarian household changes the plan more than a simple preference does. Name what cannot appear, and be clear about cross-contact concerns if they matter to you.

Beyond restrictions, share the broader eating style you want. If you are leaning toward more produce and lighter proteins, a menu like the vegetarian direction can shape the week. If you want leaner coastal flavors, the Key West inspired menu is a useful reference point.

3. What you actually enjoy eating

A meal plan nobody wants to eat is a wasted week. Tell the chef what your household genuinely enjoys — favorite proteins, cuisines, spice level, and the dishes you are tired of. Weekly prep is most useful when it reduces decisions, and that happens fastest when the menu reflects your taste.

Also mention what you can cook yourself, if anything. If you are comfortable with a few weeknight basics, the chef can focus on the meals you would otherwise skip.

4. Kitchen, storage and reheating

How the meals are stored and reheated shapes how they are prepared. Share what you are working with: fridge and freezer space, whether you have a microwave or prefer the oven, and whether you have a set of containers you want reused.

If you are reheating at an office or on the road, say so. That changes how dishes are packed and how they hold up a day or two later.

5. Schedule, delivery and lead time

Agree on the weekly rhythm early: which day meals arrive, how far ahead you need to confirm the week's plan, and how changes are handled. A consistent day makes the service easier to plan around, and knowing the lead time prevents last-minute scrambling.

Also confirm whether the chef cooks in your home or delivers prepared meals. Both approaches have their own rhythm, and the logistics matter for a busy household.

6. Budget and scope

Share a budget range so the plan can be built around it. Weekly preparation is priced by the number of meals, servings, and ingredients involved, so a clear range helps the chef propose something that fits without back-and-forth. If your priorities change week to week, agree on how flexible the plan can be.
