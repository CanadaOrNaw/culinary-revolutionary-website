A wild game dinner is the most adventurous evening on the calendar, and it works best when the table is ready for it. Culinary Revolutionary serves game as a customizable four-course Wild Game direction built around meats such as venison, bison, elk, boar, duck and rabbit, for gatherings on South Florida's Gulf Coast. Here is how to plan a game night that feels bold for the enthusiasts and welcoming for everyone else.

1. Match the menu to the table's adventurousness

Start by sorting your guests honestly. A few will have ordered venison in restaurants for years; others have never knowingly eaten game. The evening works when both groups get something to look forward to. The Wild Game starters make this easy: a Venison Carpaccio with juniper vinaigrette and pickled blackberry for the bold, a Smoked Rabbit Rillette with cherry preserves and toasted brioche for the cautious, a Duck Breast Pastrami on rye crostini that meets hesitant eaters halfway. Name the mix when you inquire so the courses can lean toward discovery or reassurance as needed.

Ask about firsts, not just restrictions. A guest who has never tried game often just needs to know what to expect: lean meat, deeper flavor than beef, nothing strange about it. The chef shapes descriptions and portion emphasis around that answer. If half the table is brand new to game, say so — it changes which dishes anchor the evening, and that is normal planning information, not a problem.

2. Pace the rich courses with space and contrast

Game courses eat richer than their size suggests, so the evening needs breathing room between them. Plan unhurried gaps, lighter accompaniments, and something bright on the plate — pickled fruit, sharp greens, citrus — to reset the palate. A four-course game menu is a full evening by design; resist the urge to add extra plates on top of it. When the table includes guests who skip meat, dishes from the Vegetarian direction keep them part of the same evening rather than an afterthought.

Tell the chef how your group eats at home. A table that usually finishes dinner in forty minutes needs a different rhythm than one that lingers for three hours. Late arrivals, kids' bedtimes and toasts all shape the pacing the same way they do for any holiday private dinner — the courses follow the evening's program, not the other way around.

3. Flag dietary needs and sourcing questions early

Game menus and dietary restrictions intersect more than guests expect, so raise both together at the start. A gluten restriction changes the crostini and crisps across several starters; a dairy allergy changes the aioli, crema and cheese that run through the menu. The dietary planning guide covers what to share and when. One guest's needs can shape the whole table's menu, and planners see that every week.

Bring sourcing questions to the inquiry too. Guests sometimes ask where game meats come from and how they are handled — fair questions with straightforward answers the chef can give. If knowing the provenance matters to your table, say so early and it becomes part of the menu conversation rather than a game-day surprise.

4. What to send when you inquire

A useful wild game inquiry names the Wild Game direction, your date or week, your city or ZIP code, your guest count or range, and the adventurousness of the table — who has eaten game before, who is trying it for the first time, and who needs a non-game plate. Add dietary needs and the shape of the evening, the same details the availability guide asks for.
